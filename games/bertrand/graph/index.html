Why moving first changes the price

← Back to the game

Simultaneous (Bertrand) vs. Boeing as the leader (Stackelberg), with the game’s default numbers: demand Q = 400 − 2·Pown + Prival, marginal cost 232 for both firms.

1. The best responses cross at Nash each firm’s best price, given the other’s

Boeing: PB = 216 + PA/4 Airbus: PA = 216 + PB/4
  1. Simultaneous. Neither firm can move the other’s price, so each one stays on its own line. The only point where both are happy is where the lines cross: 288 and 288, profit 6,272 each.
  2. Boeing leads. Boeing knows Airbus will answer along the orange line, so Boeing picks the point on Airbus’s line that is best for itself: 292, and Airbus answers 289. Boeing is now off its own line.

2. Boeing’s profit at each price it could charge

Simultaneous: Airbus’s price stays at 288 Boeing leads: Airbus answers 216 + PB/4 Collusion: Airbus matches Boeing’s price

All three curves meet at 288: there Airbus charges 288 whatever the story. They differ in how much Airbus follows when Boeing raises its price: not at all (simultaneous), $1 for every $4 (leader), or one for one (collusion). The more Airbus follows, the fewer customers Boeing loses, and the higher Boeing’s best price.

Why does the peak move right? When Boeing raises its price by $4, Airbus raises its own by $1. Airbus’s higher price sends some customers back to Boeing, so a price increase costs Boeing fewer sales. Put Airbus’s answer into Boeing’s demand:

QB = 400 − 2PB + (216 + PB/4) = 616 − 1.75·PB

Boeing’s sales now fall by 1.75 per dollar instead of 2. Less price-sensitive demand means a higher price: maximising (PB − 232)(616 − 1.75·PB) gives 292 and profit 6,300.

The leader can never do worse than Nash: Boeing could always choose 288, Airbus would answer 288, and Boeing would get 6,272 again. It moves only because something better is available.

3. The same thing with iso-profit curves zoomed in

Boeing’s best response Airbus’s best response Boeing earns 6,272 / 6,300 on these curves Nash (288, 288): 6,272 Boeing leads (292, 289): 6,300

Each dotted curve joins the price pairs that give Boeing the same profit; curves higher up pay Boeing more, because a higher Airbus price helps Boeing. At Nash, Boeing’s curve is flat: against a fixed 288, 288 is the best it can do. As the leader, Boeing slides along Airbus’s line to the highest curve that still touches it, at (292, 289).

Who gains more? Airbus, the follower: 6,498 against Boeing’s 6,300. In price competition a higher price helps the rival, and the follower slightly undercuts the leader (289 vs. 292). So here moving second is the advantage, the opposite of quantity (Cournot/Stackelberg) competition.

Collusion pays both firms the most, because they act like one firm and set the monopoly price together: maximising (P − 232)(400 − P) gives 316.

Why the cartel is fragile. In chart 1, the collusion point (316, 316) is on neither best-response line. If Boeing charges 316, Airbus’s best answer is 216 + 316/4 = 295, which earns Airbus (295 − 232)(400 − 590 + 316) = 7,938, more than 7,056. Each firm is tempted to undercut, which is why collusion needs enforcement (or repeated play) to hold.
